Disruptive Innovation Theory

In short

Disruptive innovation explains how small entrants with simpler, cheaper offerings can topple strong incumbents: they start with customers the leaders ignore, then improve until they are good enough for the mainstream. Incumbents lose not through laziness but because chasing their most profitable customers is rational. Not every new technology is disruptive; the path from the bottom or from non-consumers is what defines it.

Worked example

An accounting firm has 400 clients: 60 mid-sized companies paying $1,500 a month and 340 sole traders paying $120. Low-end foothold: a bookkeeping app offers sole traders automatic tax filing for $15 a month; it cannot handle payroll or audits. Incumbent response: the partners see the sole traders as their least profitable 15% of revenue and happily let 90 of them go in a year. Improvement trajectory: the app adds payroll and multi-user access, and two of the mid-sized clients start a trial. Good enough: for firms with under 20 staff, the app now covers 80% of what they pay the accountant for. Seeing the pattern, the partners create a separate low-price unit that runs on the same app and sells advisory hours on top, instead of defending the old fee model.

Common mistakes

  • Labelling every successful startup disruptive: a product that beats incumbents by being better and more expensive for their best customers is a sustaining innovation, not a disruption.
  • Dismissing the entrant because its product is worse today: track how fast its performance improves against what mainstream customers actually need.
  • Trying to fight the disruptor from inside the core business: the core's margins and customers will starve the new effort, so give it a separate unit with its own cost structure.

Check yourself

Answer in your head, then open the answer.

  1. A new airline offers business-class seats with better food at a higher price than the incumbent. Is this disruptive?

    No. It targets the incumbent's best customers with a better product, which is sustaining innovation that incumbents usually fight off.

  2. Why do well-managed incumbents often ignore disruptors?

    The disruptor's first market is small and low-margin, so listening to their best customers and protecting margins rationally leads leaders away from it.

  3. A cheap portable ultrasound device is first bought by rural clinics that had no imaging at all. Which type of disruption is this?

    New-market disruption: it competes against non-consumption, serving people who previously had no product.

Origin

Clayton Christensen of Harvard Business School introduced the idea with Joseph Bower in the 1995 Harvard Business Review article "Disruptive Technologies: Catching the Wave", based on his study of the disk drive industry. His 1997 book "The Innovator's Dilemma" made it famous, and "The Innovator's Solution" (2003, with Michael Raynor) renamed it disruptive innovation and added new-market disruption. In a 2015 HBR article Christensen and co-authors warned that the term was being misused.

Core idea

Incumbents improve products faster than most customers need, overshooting the mainstream. That leaves room at the bottom for simpler, cheaper or more convenient offerings that incumbents find unattractive. The entrant earns a living there, improves along its own trajectory, and eventually meets mainstream needs while keeping its lower cost. By the time incumbents react, the entrant's business model is better suited to the new market.

Key components

Sustaining innovation: improvements for existing demanding customers, which incumbents usually win. Low-end disruption: an entrant serves overserved customers with a cheaper, good-enough product. New-market disruption: an entrant reaches non-consumers who lacked money or skill to use existing products. Performance trajectories: the rate at which the entrant's product improves compared with what customers can absorb. Asymmetric motivation: incumbents are drawn upmarket by margins, entrants are happy with segments incumbents leave.

How to apply it

1) Plot your customers by how much of your product's performance they actually use. 2) Find the overserved group or the non-consumers who are priced or skilled out. 3) Watch entrants that serve them and measure how quickly they improve. 4) If you are the entrant, start where incumbents are glad to leave and build a cost structure they cannot match. 5) If you are the incumbent, launch a separate unit with its own profit targets to compete there.

Limitations

Critics, including historian Jill Lepore in a 2014 New Yorker essay, argued that Christensen chose supportive cases and that many "disrupted" incumbents recovered. The theory explains disruptions well in hindsight but gives weak signals in advance about which entrants will succeed. Its popularity has stretched the word "disruptive" to cover almost any innovation, which blurs its meaning.
